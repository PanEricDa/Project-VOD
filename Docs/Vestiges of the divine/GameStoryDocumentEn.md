This is an age of peace.

Three hundred years have passed since the fires of the Divine War were extinguished across the continent of Edalas, from the frozen peaks of the North to the emerald isles of the South. The people have long forgotten that the horizon once burned crimson. War has dwindled to a handful of lines in the history books, tucked between the coronation of some long-dead king and the record of a plague—a faded memory no one cares to stir.

It seems the peoples of this land have all but lost interest in tracing that myth-shrouded past: six ancient gods, divided between the Celestial Covenant and the Abyssal Host, waged a war that stretched across eight thousand years—the Age of Divinities. What sparked the war, what end it sought—none can now say. All that has come down through the ages is a single threadbare sentence: one day, the six gods suddenly signed an armistice. And just like that, the war was over.

Under the long peace, titles such as "warrior" and "hero" slowly faded from the common memory. The trades that had thrived on war—knightly orders, adventurers' guilds, temple militias—crumbled into dust. The sons of knights became caravan escorts, or doormen at the gates of merchant guildhalls. The heirs of archmages retreated into the academy, rising no higher than scholar or lecturer. The bloodlines of rangers scattered into the wilds, reduced to hunters and wilderness guides. Countless more heroes—and the children of heroes—simply laid down their blades and dissolved into the smoke of everyday hearths and market streets.

And yet, what the gods left behind has not departed. Sacred relics in the depths of ruined temples still murmur their whispered invocations. Broken war-golems on ancient battlefields still march to patrol orders issued eight centuries ago. Across the plains scorched to ash, something nameless still roams and wanders. These things know nothing of peace. They know only that the war is not over—that they are still waiting for an order that shall never come.

Someone has to deal with these remnants. And so a fringe trade quietly took shape—those unwilling to relinquish their weapons banded together in twos and threes, venturing into the world's forgotten corners to extract whatever relics still held value, and to press back into slumber whatever ought never to have stirred. The profession commands little respect, yet none dare claim it is unnecessary. Its practitioners call themselves the "Heritage Asset Management Authority（HAMA）."

The common folk have a more forthright name for them—

"Scavengers."
